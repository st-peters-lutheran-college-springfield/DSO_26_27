Resource 3: Visual Communication Elements & Principles

IA1 direct-teaching resource · Next Bus, Made Simple · Used in Lesson 13

15–20 minute segmentConcept first, then apply to your device
Start here: learn the concept in general terms first, then use the Next Bus examples to connect it to your own device.
Syllabus (Digital Solutions 2025 v1.4): “Explore and use the elements and principles of visual communication — elements are limited to space, line, colour, shape, texture, tone, form, proportion and scale; principles are limited to balance, contrast, proximity, harmony, alignment, repetition and hierarchy” (Topic 1); “Generate low-fidelity user-interface prototypes… using the elements and principles of visual communication” (Topic 3).

1. Elements: the Raw Ingredients of Any Visual Design

ElementOn your device's 1.54″ display
SpaceHow much empty margin surrounds the route number and time. Too little feels cramped and is hard to read quickly.
LineDividers separating the route number from the arrival time.
ColourUsing colour to distinguish ‘on time’ (for example, green) from ‘delayed’ (for example, amber or red) — but never colour alone (accessibility).
ShapeRounded status icons compared with sharp warning triangles for alerts.
Texture / toneA light or dark background tone, affecting readability in bright sunlight at a bus stop.
FormThe 3D physical shape of the device itself and how it is held or mounted.
Proportion / scaleMaking the arrival time the largest element on screen, since it is the single most important piece of information.

2. Principles: How the Elements Are Arranged

PrincipleOn your device's display
BalanceInformation isn't crowded onto one side of the small screen.
ContrastText colour against the background meets a readable contrast ratio, especially outdoors.
ProximityRelated information (route and destination) is grouped together; unrelated information is kept apart.
HarmonyIcons and text style feel consistent across every display state (on time, delayed, alert).
AlignmentText and icons line up on a consistent grid rather than floating randomly.
RepetitionThe same icon or colour always means the same status, every time it appears.
HierarchyThe most time-critical information (minutes until arrival) is the most visually dominant element.

3. Applying This to Your Sketches

When you symbolise your display and voice-interaction states in Lesson 13, annotate at least three elements and two principles directly on your sketch. For example, label where you used colour, hierarchy, and contrast, and explain why in one sentence each.

These annotations are what the marking guide's symbolisation and explanation criteria look for — not just a neat drawing.

Check for Understanding

Sketch two versions of your ‘on time’ display state: one that ignores hierarchy (everything the same size) and one that uses it. Which is faster to read at a glance, and why?
