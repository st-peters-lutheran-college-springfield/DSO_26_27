1. The Five Useability Principles (QCAA)

PrincipleWhat to check
AccessibilityCan a person with reduced vision, hearing, or dexterity still use it? (For example: large text, an audio alternative, big buttons.)
EffectivenessDoes it actually let the user complete the task — finding out whether their bus is on time?
SafetyDoes it avoid causing distress, confusion, or unsafe behaviour (for example, someone stepping into traffic to check a screen)?
UtilityDoes it provide the functions the user actually needs, without unnecessary extras?
LearnabilityCan a first-time user work it out without training or a manual?

2. Designing for Older, Less Digitally Confident Users

General useability advice for older adults and users with low digital literacy, useful when you justify your own design decisions:

  • Text size and contrast: a minimum of about 16 px equivalent font size and high colour contrast (WCAG guidance suggests at least 4.5:1 for body text).
  • Always label icons with text; don't leave them to speak for themselves.
  • Avoid splitting a task across several screens or states that require the user to remember what happened earlier.
  • Keep navigation simple, with an obvious way back to a ‘home’ or default state.
  • Provide an audio alternative for users who can't easily read a screen — directly relevant to your speaker-based output.
Source: adapted from Toptal, ‘A Guide to Interface Design for Older Adults’ (toptal.com/designers/ui/ui-design-for-older-adults), accessed September 2026. This is informal design guidance, not a QCAA source: use the five QCAA principles above as your assessed framework and this list as supporting justification.
